## Core Takeaway

In this speech, Federal Reserve Governor Michael S. Barr delivers a detailed and forceful critique of the ongoing deregulation of the U.S. banking sector. He argues that a series of recent regulatory proposals and supervisory changes—including reductions in capital requirements for the largest banks (a cumulative 6% reduction, or $60 billion less in loss-absorbing capital), weakened stress tests, diluted leverage ratios, and a 'grade inflation' in supervisory ratings—are collectively undermining the safety and soundness of the financial system. Barr frames deregulation as a form of 'underinsurance,' where short-term economic gains (a 'sugar high') are traded for a significantly higher probability of a devastating financial crisis. He supports his argument with extensive academic research, citing studies on the persistent GDP losses from past crises (e.g., 20-60% of GDP from the Global Financial Crisis), the role of capital in preventing crises, and the asymmetric costs of getting capital requirements 'too low.' He also highlights growing risks from the interconnected nonbank sector, arguing that the solution is stronger regulation of nonbanks, not weaker regulation of banks. The speech concludes with a stark warning that the current deregulatory path will make the financial system less robust and that the costs will eventually be borne by the public.
