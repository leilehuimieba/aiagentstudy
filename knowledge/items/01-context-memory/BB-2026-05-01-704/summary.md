## Core Takeaway

Hua Hsu's essay explores the curious career of the American Dream, a phrase coined by historian James Truslow Adams during the Great Depression in his book “The Epic of America.” Originally a vision of a society where everyone could reach their fullest potential regardless of birth, it became a national creed and global brand. Hsu examines how the dream was both realized and betrayed: postwar economic expansion made upward mobility a reality for many, but racial subjugation and growing inequality exposed its limits. He revisits the 1965 Baldwin-Buckley debate, the changing meaning of success in the 1960s, and the decline of economic mobility since the 1970s (Chetty's research shows the likelihood of outearning one's parents fell from 92% to 50%). The article also reports on contemporary indicators of disillusionment—Pew polling, Gen Z's modest hopes, the rise of crypto and prediction markets, and even the trend of Americans moving abroad or fetishizing China. Hsu visits the Milken Center for Advancing the American Dream, a museum of exceptionalism, and reflects on the gap between its polished narrative and the reality of a militarized D.C. and ongoing immigration crackdowns. Finally, he describes teaching a class on the American Dream, where students grapple with whether the dream still means anything and what alternative visions might replace it. The essay concludes by returning to Adams's original emphasis on collective self-improvement and the open reading room of the Library of Congress, suggesting that the dream's future lies in learning to dream differently.
