           More teams, more entertainment.

           The 2026 World Cup’s expansion to 48 teams has delivered so far, with goals flying in at a rate of 2.95 per game, the highest since 1970. But has this extra entertainment come at a cost to competitiveness?

           A cynical view is that this goal surge is a natural consequence of a diluted field, one that has produced more high-scoring, one-sided games. According to FIFA’s rankings, introduced in 1992, this tournament has the lowest average quality on record, with the average team ranked 32.4 — the first time that figure has climbed above 30.

           

           On the surface, that cynicism appears justified. The average winning margin at this World Cup stands at 2.3 goals, higher than in the group stages of each of the previous six tournaments, with 25 per cent of games so far won by a margin of three or more goals.

           

           But these surface-level statistics mask the captivating underdog stories that have brought a sense of colour and vibrancy to this World Cup. Chief among them are Cape Verde, who have emerged as the group-stage heroes after rousing performances against two former World Cup winners.

           The African island nation, the smallest country by landmass ever to play at a World Cup, began their first tournament by mounting a courageous resistance against Spain, holding the European champions to a 0-0 draw as their 40-year-old goalkeeper Vozinha pulled off a string of miraculous saves. They then backed that up with a 2-2 draw against Uruguay, a side ranked 51 places above them.

           The table below shows the largest ranking gaps in World Cup history for teams that avoided defeat, with both of Cape Verde’s results sitting inside the top 15. They are joined by four other results from this tournament, including Ghana holding England to a 0-0 draw and Curacao doing the same against Ecuador, while South Africa’s 2-1 victory in 2010 over a France side ranked 74 places above them tops the pile.

           
            

            Curacao best captures why averages only tell part of the story, having secured that momentous Ecuador draw just days after their 7-1 drubbing at the hands of Germany.

           

           After this run of headline underdog results, it is tempting to conclude that the floor of international football has been raised, and that games once considered formalities, where big sides could rest key players and still win at a canter, are now stern tests.

           There is also a much simpler explanation: this expanded World Cup has given minnows a bigger platform, creating more chances for them to spring a surprise. The graphic below shows the frequency of World Cup games, split by the ranking gap between the two sides, with a sharp rise in large ranking mismatches.

           

           Between 1994 and 2022, there were just six World Cup matches where the ranking difference was greater than 50. By the end of the group stage, there will have been eight at this tournament alone.

           Again, the chart below splits matches by ranking gap, this time looking at the percentage of games in which the lower-ranked side avoids defeat. Rank outsiders are performing slightly better, with teams ranked 60 or more places below their opponent securing a win or draw in 50 per cent of such games, but the broader truth is that underdogs have always been competitive at World Cups.

           

           Upsets at this World Cup have been largely limited to draws, with lower-ranked sides frustrating established heavyweights such as England and Spain, but rarely advancing their own cause in the way a statement win would. Ghana and South Africa are the only sides to beat a team ranked 30 or more places above them, and their victories over Panama and South Korea, respectively, were hardly seismic shocks.

           Compare that with the 2002 tournament, where Senegal’s 1-0 opening-day victory over France, and South Korea’s upset over Portugal in their final group game by the same scoreline, sparked deep runs into the knockout stages for both sides.

           These were two of seven games in that tournament where the ranking gap was above 30, and while the number of those mismatches was smaller, underdogs both won and avoided defeat at a higher rate than in equivalent matches at this tournament. The table below shows that, across World Cup tournaments, underdogs avoid defeat roughly once every three encounters.

           

           When the dust settles on the 2026 World Cup, it is likely that the tournament minnows will have caused minor disturbances in the early stages rather than leaving a lasting impression on the knockout stages. Fears that they would be swallowed up by the established favourites, though, have proved well wide of the mark.

           History shows that World Cup minnows have always been capable of battling admirably against the odds, and the class of 2026 is no exception.
