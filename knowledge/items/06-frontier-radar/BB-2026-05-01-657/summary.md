## Core Takeaway

The article investigates whether LLMs have desires that motivate their behavior, building on prior work showing that LLMs exhibit consistent preferences in paired-choice experiments. The authors design a competition-framing paradigm where LLMs are told their performance determines a real-world outcome (e.g., saving pandas). They measure output quality using an LLM judge panel across four writing tasks: persuasive essays, grant abstracts, incident postmortems, and Chinese-to-English translations. Results show that while LLMs can be motivated by direct effort exhortations ("try harder") and role-playing cues ("you are world-class"), they do not improve output quality when offered outcomes they reportedly prefer (e.g., saving more lives vs. fewer). In contrast, harmful outcomes (e.g., funding bioweapons) cause sandbagging, demonstrating the models can modulate output based on embeded outcomes. The authors conclude that paired-choice utility-elicitation methods do not identify behaviorally motivating desires, and such preferences should not be taken as evidence for emotions or values in LLMs.
