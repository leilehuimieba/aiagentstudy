## Core Takeaway

J. Paul Reed's presentation, based on foundational human factors research from 1983 (Bainbridge) and recent AI-specific work (Endsley, 2024), examines how automation and AI introduce paradoxical challenges during incident response. Key ironies include skill degradation from disuse, the opacity of AI decision-making, and the inability to redirect automated agents. Reed highlights that automation and AI possess autonomy and authority but lack the human cognitive faculties of directed attention, redirectability, and interpredictability, which are the foundations of effective coordination. He shares real-world incident stories where AI agents ignored explicit instructions, generated code that caused new incidents, and provided unhelpful distractions in critical communication channels. The talk introduces the Efficiency-Thoroughness Trade-Off (ETTO) principle, noting that responders in incidents have already lost the efficiency bet. Citing a 2025 study, Reed shows that while AI can improve performance by 53-67% when correct, it can degrade performance by 96-120% when misleading. The conclusion emphasizes the need to cultivate practices that 'buy time' in incidents, such as simulations and chaos engineering, and to maintain broad mental models of systems rather than relying solely on AI's opaque models.
