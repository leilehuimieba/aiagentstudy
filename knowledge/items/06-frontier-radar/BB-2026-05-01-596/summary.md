## Core Takeaway

Using a rewatch of Pickpocket as a starting point, the article systematically examines Bresson's theory of "cinematography." The author argues that Bresson was more radical than the French New Wave, striving to strip cinema from theater by removing actors' expressions and reducing psychological description, allowing material elements like hands and objects to directly convey meaning. It focuses on the four-and-a-half-minute pickpocketing sequence at the train station, showing how close-ups of hands replace faces as the narrative core. The article further uses Crime and Punishment as a reference to reveal a profound shift after Bresson eliminates the protagonist's inner life: crime transforms from philosophically driven to a physical addiction. The conclusion notes that Bresson's repression-release structure influenced Schrader (American Gigolo, Taxi Driver), but Bresson refuses to excuse social factors, making Pickpocket colder and more honest. The argument is solid, the perspective unique, showcasing the depth of film criticism.
